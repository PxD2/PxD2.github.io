Lee: Did our militia belong to Washington? I do not want a king in a wig. I want to know who went home.

Bell: They belonged to the law and to North Carolina. They answered when the Continental line needed a crossing held. Davidson held fords so Greene could move. Polk wintered at Valley Forge with Washington’s army. That is in the North Carolina Valley Forge story: the nineteenth of December, 1777, the North Carolina brigade walked into the snow, and young William Polk was there with a hurt mouth from Germantown, teeth gone, still a major.

Vee: Valley Forge sounds like a sad song. Was it only sad?

Bell: It was a hard winter and a loyal one. Huts. Hunger. A commander who stayed with the men. A king stays on a throne. A president goes home. That is the first lesson of presidents in this show. Energy in the office, not a crown in the man. Washington had energy. He did not keep a crown.

Lee: Did he come to Charlotte? Would I have hidden under the table?

Bell: Years later, yes. Colonel Thomas Polk hosted Washington on the southern tour. The same Polk who had hauled bells. Imagine: the commander at your table, and you had already saved a bell for him without asking for a statue. You would bake too many biscuits. That is correct. Love is biscuits, not marble.

Vee: I would bake too many biscuits. I would save him the chair nearest the fire.

Lee: I would save Lafayette a biscuit too, if he came later.

Bell: William Polk’s mouth was hurt at Germantown, the fourth of October 1777, while he shouted to his men. The North Carolina story at Valley Forge says he spent the winter getting well among the huts. He still went on — Cowan’s Ford, Guilford, Eutaw. When he was old he was the last field officer of the North Carolina Line left alive. He ate breakfast with Lafayette in Raleigh, the same Lafayette who had been a boy at Brandywine with him. That is how you love Washington’s world: you survive the snow, you go home, you keep a table for a friend.

Vee: A president goes home. Copy that. Recite it when anyone wants a king.

Bell: Yes. Our people did not wait to be asked. They held a ford. They hauled a bell. They sat in snow. Then they went home and kept courts and colleges. Washington is in this show because he left. That is the opposite of a fairy-tale king. Copy: a president goes home. Recite it in a meeting house voice, which is quieter than a parade.

Lee: A president goes home. Energy in the office, not a crown in the man.

Bell: Good. Picture the nineteenth of December: North Carolina walking into snow. Picture a hurt mouth learning to speak again in a hut. Picture a Charlotte table years later, too many biscuits, no statue requested. Picture Washington leaving the chair. That is the whole presidency lesson in a meeting house.

Bell: Save both biscuits. Then go home yourself. Sit at Valley Forge one more minute without making it a song only. Huts in rows. A hurt mouth learning words again. North Carolina in the snow on the nineteenth of December. A commander who could have taken a warmer house and did not. That is energy in the office. That is not a crown. Years later a Charlotte table, Thomas Polk hosting the southern tour, too many biscuits, no statue requested. William still carrying Germantown in his jaw and still keeping a table for Lafayette in Raleigh. Survive, go home, keep a table. That is the lesson.

Vee: I will not make him a cloud. I will make him a guest. I will still bake too many biscuits.

Lee: I will hang the coat after the snow. Coats dry. Crowns do not belong on pegs. A president goes home.

Bell: Copy: a president goes home. Recite: energy in the office, not a king in the man. Ring once. Tomorrow Madison’s notebook, the yoke I hang from, and two kinds of cousins arguing without becoming monsters. Bring a pencil. I will bring the bell and the yoke and I will not let you skip either.
