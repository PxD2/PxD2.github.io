Bell: Speak softly. This is the horse episode. We do not draw a shot. We draw a barn door, and a horse coming home without a rider. If you remember only one picture from this whole show, let it be that horse.

Lee: The coat is heavy today. Tell me about the man who wore it first, like he could still borrow a cup of something.

Bell: William Lee Davidson was a militia general. Valley Forge. Germantown. He married a Brevard. His father’s will named John Brevard and Alexander Osborne as guardians of the children — Adlai Osborne’s house. One Davidson family, not two. He was uncle to Major John of Rural Hill, and uncle in Adlai’s house. Uncles are how this nest holds.

Vee: The horse was from Rural Hill. A nephew’s horse.

Bell: Major John lent his uncle a horse from the nephew’s barn, to ride to the Catawba, to hold Cowan’s Ford against Cornwallis, the first of February, 1781. Fog on the river. The general fell. The horse came home. That is how Major John knew. They laid William Lee at Hopewell in a hurry, so the other army would not find him. Friends in the dark. A meeting house, not a monument.

Lee: Did the college know? How does a foggy river become a school?

Bell: The college is the love letter. The general’s own baby — William Lee the second — was born when his father was already becoming a story. He later married Betsy Lee Davidson, Major John’s girl. They built a house called Beaver Dam. On the thirteenth of May, 1835, a Presbyterian committee sat at that house at candlelight, after solemn and special prayer, and chose land for a school. Four hundred sixty-nine acres, two miles east of the house, some in Mecklenburg, some in Iredell.

Vee: Candlelight. I can see the table. Did they pass a plate?

Bell: They passed a purpose. In August they named the school Davidson College as a tribute to the memory of that distinguished and excellent man, General William Davidson, who fell, universally lamented, at Cowan’s Ford. The son sold the land — some say later gave it back in love. In 1839 he endowed a professorship with two thousand dollars. He sat vice-president of the first board, and treasurer. Students arrived in March 1837 and hauled debris and built fences. So every student is walking on a thank-you.

Lee: So the horse came home, and a college stood up.

Bell: Yes. Copy that sentence. Recite it without shouting. Major John, old, died at Beaver Dam in 1832 and was carried back to Rural Hill. The archives at Davidson still hold this house. When you see the college name, you are seeing a foggy river, a borrowed horse, and a boy who would not let his father be only a rumor.

Vee: The horse is my favorite character after Aunt Susie. The horse has no lines.

Bell: That is why we love him. He came home. We rang. We built a school. Lee, you may keep the coat. It will fit when your love is as big as the sleeves. Be an uncle worth a college by first being a cousin worth a horse.

Lee: I will not let him be a rumor. I will try to be an uncle worth a college. I will start by being a cousin worth a horse.

Bell: Davidson’s archives will not hug you. I will. If you go, ask for Beaver Dam and the general. Ask for the candlelight minute. Ask why a manual labor school took a fallen uncle’s name. None of that is showing off. It is a family keeping a promise in brick and books.

Vee: I will copy: the horse came home, and a college stood up.

Lee: I will recite: one Davidson family. Uncle to Major John. Uncle to Adlai. Not two lines. One.

Bell: Good. Hang the coat gently. Ring once. Sit with the horse. That is enough for today. Tomorrow Graham in the pines, sheriff not governor, lantern still lit. Good night, Lee. Good night, Vee. The ford is quiet. The school is awake.
