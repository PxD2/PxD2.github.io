Vee: Aunt Susie again. I want the whole lantern, and the man she carried, and the boy who later sat in a chair.

Bell: Joseph Graham, born the thirteenth of October 1759 in Pennsylvania, raised on this river. Fifteen fights, private to major. Charlotte, the twenty-sixth of September 1780: hurt nine times, left, found by Susan Alexander hunting cows. Two months to stand. Then he raised a company of fifty-five in two weeks. His own papers say so — the declaration in the Colonial Records, volume twenty-two, Chapel Hill. Later his son William A. Graham printed the book: General Joseph Graham and His Papers on North Carolina Revolutionary History, 1904. That is a son loving a father with ink.

Lee: Was he governor? I always mix them up.

Bell: No. And that is how you tell the truth, which is how you love him. Joseph was first sheriff of Mecklenburg, a senator, a justice of the peace for forty years, a brigadier in 1814, major general of militia, elder at Unity Church. He made iron. He sat as councillor of state. The governor’s chair waited for his boy. William Alexander Graham: Governor of North Carolina, United States Senator, Secretary of the Navy, Whig vice-presidential nominee in 1852. Father filled the pines. Son filled the chair. Do not mix up their jobs.

Vee: I like that the father stayed a neighbor. Sheriffs know everyone’s dogs.

Bell: He wrote accounts for Archibald Murphey, who wanted a state history. The book was never finished. Graham’s pages became the archive. That is how UNC still knows the nest. A man who had been a boy at the courthouse wrote it down so you would not have to guess. If you ever walk the North Carolina collection at Chapel Hill, you are walking through a father’s memory that a son refused to lose.

Lee: Did he marry Isabella? The hinge from Rural Hill?

Bell: Isabella Davidson, Major John’s daughter. So the lantern and the horse and the college are one house. Copy: sheriff, not governor. Recite: a cousin hunting cows. Then you have loved him correctly, not as a stuffed angel. Aunt Susie was not mustered. She was better. She looked where a person had fallen and she took him home.

Vee: Ring for her. I will draw the lantern larger than any sword.

Bell: After the war he still found time to write those pages. A militia general who became a historian of his own youth. Picture him at seventy-three in a Lincoln County court, swearing he has a record of his age, swearing he was present in Charlotte on the twentieth of May 1775 as a looker-on. Picture a son typesetting those sentences in 1904. Love them both.

Lee: Sheriff father. Governor son. I can keep that. I will write it inside the coat.

Vee: Lantern cousin. Plow in the field. Ink in Chapel Hill. I can keep that.

Bell: Then Graham is alive in you. That is all a paper wants. Sit with him one more minute as a neighbor, not a caption. Picture Unity Church on a Sunday. An elder with saber scars under a clean shirt. Picture a sheriff who knows which ford is up. Picture iron cooling. Picture Murphey asking for a state history and Graham sending pages instead of boasts. Picture a son in 1904 setting type and refusing to lose a plow, a lantern, a courthouse, a looker-on in May of seventy-five.

Lee: I will not mix their jobs even if someone else does. Sheriff in the pines. Governor in the chair.

Vee: I will keep the lantern larger than the chair. That is how a child loves a father and a son without stacking them into one pretend man.

Bell: The Southern Historical Collection will not make him a knight. Neither will we. Copy tonight: Joseph Graham, sheriff, senator, justice, militia general — not governor. Recite: his son William A. Graham was Governor of North Carolina, Senator, Secretary of the Navy. Ring for the pines. Ring for Unity Church. Ring for a boy who left a plow and an old man who would not decorate. Tomorrow two cousins walk out of Nassau Hall with the same year on their names. Bring your hats. I will bring the brick.
