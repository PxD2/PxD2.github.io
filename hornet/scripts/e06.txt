Lee: Nassau Hall. Say it like a castle, then tell the truth so I do not think it is magic.

Bell: Brick. Princeton. The College of New Jersey. In 1768 two Piedmont cousins walked out of that hall with the same year on their names: Ephraim Brevard and Adlai Osborne. Crowfield Academy first, near Belmont, the Osborne house — the old red house children still told about a hundred years on. Then a private school in Virginia. Then Nassau. John Witherspoon arrived that same year from Paisley in Scotland to be president. Moral philosophy. English lectures. A republican spirit in the brick.

Vee: Did they miss home? Did they pack too many hats?

Bell: They came home on purpose. That is the courage of this episode — to learn first, then come home. Ephraim became the doctor in Charlotte. He wrote Mecklenburg’s resolves — the paper that said the king’s law was vacant here. He married a daughter of Colonel Thomas Polk. He was taken at Charleston and came home ill, and the river lost him. We do not make a game. We say: the doctor wrote, and then he was quiet, and the cousins kept the paper.

Lee: Adlai lived. Tell me the slow bravery.

Bell: Adlai became the lawyer of Rowan. Clerk of the court of pleas, 1772 until 1809 — think of that, children: thirty-seven years of ink. Committee of Safety. A trustee of the University of North Carolina. Belmont stayed a Presbyterian lamp. He married Margaret Lloyd in 1771. His son, another Ephraim Brevard Osborne, was named for the cousin. That is in the family papers, and NCpedia, and the Princetonians book that keeps Nassau’s sons.

Vee: So Davidson and UNC and Nassau are not three strangers.

Bell: One house sent boys to Nassau, then built UNC, then named Davidson for an uncle. Copy: 1768, two cousins. Recite: clerk of Rowan. Love a man who kept a docket for thirty-seven years. That is as brave as a ford, only slower. Love a doctor who wrote a resolve and then grew quiet.

Lee: I could keep a docket if the coat had pockets. I will carry the books if Vee carries the hats.

Vee: I want to walk with a cousin to a brick hall and then walk home on purpose.

Bell: Deal. We will sew you pockets. First, walk the brick in your head. Witherspoon at the door. Two Carolina boys with new hats. The trustees thought they were getting one kind of minister and got a teacher who put lectures in English and put a spirit of liberty in the hall. Madison sat in those rooms later. Ephraim and Adlai sat in them first, from this river. Nassau was never a foreign country to this nest. It was a walk with a cousin.

Lee: Copy: Nassau 1768. Recite: two cousins, one river.

Bell: Yes. If you go to Nassau, ask for Princetonians, 1748 to 1768, Brevard and Osborne. If you go to Chapel Hill, remember Adlai sat a trustee. If you go to Davidson, remember Belmont was the lamp they walked back to, near the later college. None of those rooms will hug you. I will. The paper will wait.

Vee: Learn first. Then come home. I like that order more than any battle.

Lee: My notebook will be messy. My docket will have horse drawings in the margin.

Bell: Messy with purpose is scholarship. Sit in the brick a minute longer. Nassau Hall was one of the largest buildings in the colonies. All the college lived in it — sleep, class, meals, argument. Witherspoon put lectures in English so a boy from Rocky River could carry moral philosophy home in his own mouth. Madison would sit there later. Our cousins sat there first. Adlai’s people still told children about Belmont, the old red house near what became Davidson, a hundred years on. That is how you know a school took. Not a plaque. A child still saying the house name.

Vee: I will say Belmont. I will say Crowfield. I will say two hats on a long road home.

Lee: I will say clerk of Rowan until 1809, which is a kind of horse that never comes home without its rider, because the rider is still at the desk.

Bell: Love a clerk. Love a doctor. Love a president who taught them both without knowing their river yet. If you go to Nassau, ask for Princetonians, 1748 to 1768, Brevard and Osborne. If you go to Chapel Hill, remember Adlai sat a trustee. If you go to Davidson, remember Belmont was the lamp they walked back to. None of those rooms will hug you. I will. Ring once. Tomorrow Washington goes home, which is the first presidency lesson, and a Polk of this house will have already sat in the snow with him. Bring a biscuit. I will bring the yoke.
