Vee: Madison went to the same Princeton. Did he know our cousins? Did he keep his pencil too sharp?

Bell: A few years after Ephraim and Adlai. Class of 1771, Nassau Hall, Witherspoon still in the chair. Madison kept a notebook at the Convention like a boy who knows he will have to explain it. That is why we love him: not because he won every argument, but because he wrote so the people could read the argument. A notebook is a kind of lantern.

Lee: Is the Constitution the bell or the steeple? I always mix iron.

Bell: The Declaration is why I ring. The Constitution is the yoke I hang from. Without a yoke, a bell falls. Without a reason, a yoke is only iron. Madison understood the yoke. The Anti-Federalists understood the fear of a yoke too tight. Our big hall lets both speak. This cartoon says: he wrote notes so you could see the fight was a fight of cousins, not of monsters. Publius and Brutus both get a hearing. We do not flatten either.

Vee: I want a notebook. I want to write the fight down so nobody has to become a monster.

Lee: I will lend you a page if you lend me a pencil. My notebook will be messy.

Bell: Madison’s was messy with purpose. Messy with purpose is scholarship. Witherspoon taught moral philosophy in English so a Carolina boy could carry it home. Madison carried it to a room of arguments. Copy: notebook. Recite: yoke and bell. Then you have watched Madison without turning him into a marble man.

Vee: Learn first. Then come home. Then write it down. That is three lanterns.

Bell: Witherspoon signed the Declaration. Nassau Hall even housed Congress for a moment in 1783. The brick has heard arguments and muskets and lectures. Our cousins walked out of it before the muskets, which is a kind of courage too. Madison walked out later and kept writing. If you only remember a wig, you have not done the episode. If you copy one line from a note, you have.

Bell: Yes. Copy: notebook. Recite the yoke and the bell. Sit in the room of arguments one more minute. Some men wanted a strong yoke so the wagon would not spill. Some men feared a yoke so tight the horse could not breathe. Both were cousins of a kind. Publius wrote for the yoke. Brutus wrote for the fear. Madison wrote so you could still sit at the table and see it was a fight, not a spell. Our civics hall keeps the longer fight and lets both speak. Here we only love the habit: write it down. Witherspoon at the door. Lectures in English. Nassau Hall housing Congress in 1783, brick that has heard muskets and motions. Our cousins walked out before the muskets. Madison walked out later with a notebook. If you only remember a wig, you have not done the episode. If you copy one line, you have. A small man. A large habit. A pencil that outlived a shout.

Vee: I like Brutus having a chair. Cousins argue. Then they still pass the biscuits. I will copy one line tonight. I will not skip the fear just because I like the ring.

Lee: If I cheer only one side I have skipped the notebook. Messy with purpose. Yoke and bell. Declaration why we ring. Constitution so we do not fall.

Bell: Copy: notebook. Recite: the Constitution is a form to keep the Declaration’s end. Ring once. Tomorrow a loud Carolina boy, and we will not make him a stuffed angel either. Whisper practice would not hurt. The law is king, even when the people cheer. Bring your quiet voice. I will bring Jackson, and I will not flatten him.
