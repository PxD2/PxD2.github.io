Lee: Why do people call us hornets? Did we sting somebody?

Bell: A British general found Charlotte was not a chair. On the twenty-sixth of September, 1780, Cornwallis’s army came up the street that is now Tryon. Colonel Davie had horsemen at the courthouse. Captain Joseph Graham was there — eighteen, twenty, a Mecklenburg boy. They fired, they fell back, they fired again. Cornwallis later called this country a hornet nest.

Vee: Was Graham scared? Tell me like he is a boy, not a statue.

Bell: He was brave and he was a boy. He had been born the thirteenth of October, 1759, in Chester County, Pennsylvania. When he was about ten his mother brought the children to this river. He went to Queen’s Museum in Charlotte and was a good scholar of mannerly bearing. The papers his son later printed — General Joseph Graham and His Papers, Chapel Hill keeps the trail — say he had been plowing his mother’s field when he heard the British were coming, and he left the plow. That is how you love a country: you put down the handle and you go.

Lee: I would forget my hat.

Bell: He forgot more than a hat. Near the cross roads the other horsemen caught him. He was hurt nine times — six by saber, three by lead — and left. We do not make a game of that. We say: a cousin hunting cows found him. Aunt Susie Alexander. She got him home. She washed and dressed the hurts and kept him until he could stand. Militia is a cousin with a lantern.

Vee: I love Aunt Susie. She is my favorite soldier and she was not even mustered.

Bell: Love her. Love the fifty neighbors who collected in the night when Davie rode in. Love the courthouse that was not a palace. A nest is a family. That is the joke and the truth. Two months to stand. Then he raised a company of fifty-five in two weeks, their own horses, their own swords. No wagon of biscuits. That is in his declaration.

Lee: Hornets go home. Then they come back. Did he write it down?

Bell: Yes. Listen to how he told it when he was old, seventy-three, in Lincoln County, the thirtieth of October, 1832, in open court, to obtain a pension. He enlisted in May 1778 under Captain Gooden in the Fourth North Carolina. Orderly sergeant. Quartermaster sergeant. He was in Charlotte on the twentieth of May, 1775, a boy, a looker-on, when the committee spoke, more than a year before Philadelphia. He never forgot. That is why we trust him. He was there, and he wrote it, and his son printed it in 1904.

Vee: A man who will still tell the truth fifty years later without making himself a knight.

Bell: Exactly. Copy one sentence: a nest is a family. Recite it. Then sting only what is unjust, and fly home. Chapel Hill keeps the Colonial Records, volume twenty-two, and the Southern Historical Collection keeps the Joseph Graham Papers. If you walk those rooms you are walking through a father’s memory that a son refused to lose.

Lee: I will write down today so I do not become a knight by accident.

Vee: I will draw Aunt Susie’s lantern and no swords. And a plow standing alone in a field.

Bell: Good hornets. Picture him as a lad about half grown in May of seventy-five, watching men speak. Picture him leaving a plow. Picture a lantern. Picture a court in Lincoln County and an old man who will not decorate. That is how you bring him to life. Not with a cheer. With a page.

Lee: I want to be a looker-on who later tells the truth.

Bell: Then you are already in his school. He fought in fifteen small fights, private to major. We do not list them like a drum. We keep Charlotte, the lantern, the fifty-five, the oath at seventy-three. The nest is closed for the night. Buzz softly. Copy: Cornwallis called Mecklenburg a hornet nest. Recite: the families stung and scattered and came back.

Vee: A nest is a family. Aunt Susie had the lantern.

Lee: Joseph left the plow. He did not stay a knight.

Bell: Ring twice. Sleep like hornets who know the way home. Tomorrow a horse comes home without a rider, and we will still begin with love, not with a shot.
