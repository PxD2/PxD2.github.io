Jacksonian political ideals

A class, not a shrine. Jacksonian democracy meant popular sovereignty, hostility to monopoly, rotation in office, and a hard union — and it meant an electorate of white men, Indian Removal, and a presidency that bent the Court. Watch, then read the sources. Drop your own lesson films in this folder when you have them; YouTube here is cited, not copied.

The people ruleMass parties and white-male suffrage. Consent is numbers in the open, not a caucus of the propertied.
No moneyed aristocracyThe Bank War: a national bank as privilege. Producers over speculators.
Rotation in officeOffices are not a class inheritance. Spoils as a theory of equal access — and as a machine.
The Union is the covenantNullification is refused. Jackson against Calhoun: the people of the United States, not a state convention, made the compact.
Equal rights — named, then fencedEqual protection among those admitted as the people. Women, Black Americans, and Native nations were not in that sentence. Teach the fence.
Executive energyVeto as a political tool. The magistrate as tribune. The same energy that kills a bank also ignores Worcester v. Georgia.

Primary sources (public domain)

“It is to be regretted that the rich and powerful too often bend the acts of government to their selfish purposes… When the laws undertake to add to these natural and just advantages artificial distinctions, to grant titles, gratuities, and exclusive privileges, to make the rich richer and the potent more powerful, the humble members of society — the farmers, mechanics, and laborers — who have neither the time nor the means of securing like favors to themselves, have a right to complain of the injustice of their Government.”

Andrew Jackson, Veto Message, Second Bank of the United States, 10 July 1832.

“I consider, then, the power to annul a law of the United States, assumed by one State, incompatible with the existence of the Union, contradicted expressly by the letter of the Constitution, unauthorized by its spirit, inconsistent with every principle on which it was founded, and destructive of the great object for which it was formed.”

Andrew Jackson, Proclamation Regarding Nullification, 10 December 1832.

The Court named a right in Worcester v. Georgia (1832); the executive and Georgia did not carry it. The Indian Removal Act (28 May 1830) is part of this class, not a footnote. Civic education does not sand this down.

Indian Removal Act, 1830; Worcester v. Georgia, 31 U.S. 515 (1832).
